She Glows

A Brand Built for Women Who Forgot Their Fire

She Glows wasn't created to sell wellness.

It was created to answer a question.

What if a wellness brand spoke to women the way they deserved to be spoken to?

Every decision — from the colors to the copy — was made to help women feel recognized before they ever felt marketed to.

This project became an exploration of strategy, emotion, and storytelling working together.

The Feeling Before the Brand

Before a single letterform was drawn, I wanted to understand how She Glows should feel.

Warm light. Sun-drenched pigment. Softness with fire underneath.

This became the emotional starting point for everything that followed.

The Identity

The visual identity needed to hold two things at once: fire and softness.

I wanted the brand to feel warm and alive without becoming loud, and feminine without relying on the usual wellness clichés.

I didn't choose the palette to look beautiful. I chose it to feel true.

The Words

The visual identity could create the feeling, but the words had to carry it.

I wanted She Glows to speak to women like people — not an audience segment.

The voice needed to feel encouraging without becoming cliché, powerful without becoming loud, and personal without trying too hard.

This is where your copywriting becomes proof of your philosophy, rather than just another deliverable.

The Experience

A brand shouldn't become a different brand every time someone encounters it.

So I carried the same voice and emotional language into the places where She Glows would actually meet her audience.

Email. Content. Social.

Different touchpoints. Same woman. Same feeling.

Field Study

The 60/20/17/3
Awareness Framework

I found a marketing framework on YouTube. Then I tried it on myself.

By Ruth Machar

01 Field Study

The Discovery

I wasn't looking for a new content strategy.

I came across the 60/20/17/3 awareness framework almost randomly and became curious about whether it could explain something I was seeing in my own content.

The idea was simple: people don't all encounter a brand from the same place. Some don't know they have a problem. Some know something is wrong but don't know what to do about it. Others are comparing solutions, while a small percentage are already close to taking action.

What does someone need from me at the stage they're actually in?

The framework is based on Eugene Schwartz's awareness model, which segments prospects by how aware they are of their problem and the available solution.

Level Awareness State Their Question
60%Unaware of the problem"What's wrong?"
20%Aware of the problem, not of solution"Why does this keep happening?"
17%Aware of problem and solution, evaluating options"Which one actually works?"
3%Ready to buy, needs final reassurance"Why you over someone else?"
02 Field Study

The Experiment

I stopped treating every piece of content like it had the same job.

I mapped the four awareness stages against the kind of content, hook, emotion and CTA I wanted to use.

The Hook Strategy

The hook had to meet the person before the pitch did. For the unaware audience, I used stories and characters that could make people recognise themselves first. For the problem aware audience, I moved closer to the frustration they were already feeling and gave it language. For the solution aware audience, I used frameworks and examples to show how I approached the problem. For people already aware of me, I kept the message direct.

Different awareness. Different entry point.

The CTA Strategy

The next step had to make sense too. I didn't want to put the same CTA at the end of every post. The CTA was supposed to move someone forward, not simply generate engagement. I used a lower friction invitation earlier in the journey, then moved toward deeper evaluation and, for product aware people, a direct path into conversation.

The content created the awareness. The CTA created the next step.

03 Field Study

The Results

Then I started watching what changed.

The experiment gave me movement across visibility, engagement, audience growth and conversations.

5+

Discovery calls booked through cold outreach

a direct line from awareness matched content to real conversations

Results Observed

Over less than two months

Here's What I've Noticed

Growth like this rarely comes from one lucky post. It comes from every piece of content having a job to do, matched to the person seeing it. That's a system, not a stroke of luck, and systems can be audited, refined, and repeated.

04 Field Study

The Audit

The framework worked. My definition of the audience didn't.

I had applied the 60/20/17/3 model to everyone I could reach on LinkedIn instead of defining the 100% as my actual target market.

That meant some of my content was doing exactly what I had designed it to do, attracting the unaware. The problem was that some of those people were never going to be my clients.

The framework wasn't broken. I had aimed it at the wrong 100%.

My Old Approach

  • 60% — storytelling
  • 20% — permission
  • 17% — frameworks
  • 3% — actionable steps

The Correction

  • 0% — deliberately targeting the 60%
  • 40% — naming the invisible problem
  • 50% — case studies and POV
  • 10% — direct offer and scarcity

What I Took From It

I didn't discover a magic content formula. I discovered that a framework is only as useful as the audience you apply it to. The real strategic question isn't "How do I move everyone through the funnel?" It's "Who actually belongs in my funnel?"

The patterns are useful. The application is personal.

Audit

The Conversion
Architecture Audit

Why the top 1% of brand designers convert without trying and the three sections worth auditing.

By Ruth Machar

Serex Studio · Where Serenity Meets Exclusivity

The Architecture

I didn't analyze their color palettes, typography, or logo styles. I analyzed structure, language, and psychology.

These patterns work whether you're a luxury brand designer, a wellness creative, or a packaging specialist because they're not about looking good. They're about signaling trust.

What follows are the patterns I observed. The execution, the part where your unique voice meets these structures, is where strategy becomes personal.

What This Is

An audit of structure, language, and psychology across the top 1% of converting profiles.

What This Isn't

A template. A formula that replaces your voice. A shortcut around knowing yourself.

Where It Applies

Headline, About, and Featured, the three sections doing the most silent work.

Let's go.

01 Section One

The Headline

40x

LinkedIn profiles with specific, results oriented headlines receive 40x more opportunities than those with generic job titles. Not 40% more, 40 times more.

LinkedIn is a search engine. When a potential client types "help with my brand" into the search bar, it scans your headline first. If all it finds is "Brand Designer | Logo & Identity," you're invisible.

The pattern I observed: the top 1% don't use their headline to state a job title, LinkedIn already knows that. They use it to state their value proposition.

The Formula

"I help [specific audience] achieve [specific outcome] without [specific pain]"

Real Examples From the Audit

  • "Helping wellness founders attract high value clients without burning out"
  • "Brand identity for beauty and food startups that need to stand out"
  • "For founders who want their brand to feel as premium as their product"

Why This Works

Generic headlines are forgettable. Specific headlines are searchable. When you name your audience and outcome, you're not just describing yourself, you're qualifying your ideal client. They read it and think: "That's exactly what I need."

02 Section Two

The About Section

The Authenticity Trap.

There's a common belief that being "authentic" means being casual, behind the scenes, and unfiltered. But research into 1,011 executive LinkedIn posts revealed something counterintuitive: surface level authenticity, casual tone, unpolished photos, doesn't drive trust on its own. What drives trust is credibility signals.

The pattern I observed: the top 1% don't open their About section with a resume. They open with a belief statement.

Belief Statements From the Audit

  • "Brands don't grow because they're perfectly constructed. They grow because people can feel the person behind them."
  • "Strategy gives a brand direction. Soul gives it connection."
  • "Your product is premium. Your brand should feel that way too."

The 3 Second Rule

On mobile, only the first 2 to 3 lines of your About section are visible before the reader taps "see more." The top 1% use those lines to state their philosophy, not their credentials.

Why This Works

A belief signals point of view, and point of view signals expertise. When you state a belief, you're not just informing the reader, you're filtering them. The people who agree are your people. The people who disagree were never going to hire you anyway.

Here's What I've Noticed

A belief statement only works if it's actually your belief, not a borrowed one. The designers I work with take 2 to 3 rounds to land theirs. Not because they don't know themselves, but because articulating it in a way that lands takes extraction. And extraction benefits from a second pair of eyes.

03 Section Three

The Featured Section

The Featured section is the most underutilized real estate on LinkedIn. Most designers treat it like a portfolio, pinning their most visually impressive project and calling it a day. The top 1% treat it like a storefront.

Most Profiles

A portfolio. It shows what you've made.

Top 1%

A storefront. It shows what the visitor should do next.

TypePurposeExample
One Case StudyProofProblem to Solution to Result, with numbers
One Philosophy PostBeliefYour point of view on branding
One Clear CTADirectionNewsletter, lead magnet, or booking link

Every visitor who lands on your profile is in one of two states: casual curiosity ("who is this person?") or active consideration ("could this be the person I need?"). Your Featured section has to serve both, the case study satisfies the considerer, the philosophy post builds trust with the curious browser, and the CTA gives both a next step.
